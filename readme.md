# [No-Transmitter Position] Multi-Base Station Localization Without Transmitter Location [with MATLAB Code]

The multi-base system utilizes a transmitter to illuminate the target and collects reflected signals from multiple receivers to determine the target's position. In certain scenarios, such as passive coherent localization or for flexibility, the transmitter's location is unknown. This paper investigates the problem of locating targets using only indirect path measurements from the target's reflections, or by combining direct path measurements from the transmitter to the receiver, when the transmitter's location is unknown. We show that by jointly estimating the target and transmitter's positions, utilizing both indirect and direct measurement values can eliminate the dependence on the transmitter's location, thereby achieving better target position estimation than solely relying on indirect measurements. To address the nonlinearity issues associated with joint estimation, we develop an algebraic closed-form solution and demonstrate through analysis that this solution achieves the Cramer-Rao lower bound performance within a small error region under Gaussian noise. To complete the study and gain a deeper understanding, we derive the optimal receiver layout when the transmitter's location is unknown by minimizing the estimated confidence region for the target's position or the mean squared estimate error. The performance loss due to the unknown transmitter's location under the optimal geometric structure is quantified. Simulation results align well with theoretical deductions.
Introduction: Target positioning, whether cooperative or non-cooperative, has always been a hot research area, with wide applications in wireless sensor networks (WSN), network positioning and navigation (NLN), communication, radar, and sonar among others. The goal of positioning is to estimate the target's position based on a set of noisy measurements collected by multiple receivers (sensors) in an active or passive manner. Positioning can use time-based measurements such as Time of Arrival (TOA) or Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA), or other measurements including Received Signal Strength (RSS) or Angle of Arrival (AOA), or their combinations. This work focuses on active positioning to better control performance and emphasizes time-based measurements, which typically provide higher positioning accuracy.
We consider the target to be passive, meaning it does not actively send signals to a sensor for positioning. Instead, the transmitter that can mark the time of transmission emits a detection signal, and multiple synchronized receivers collect echoes reflected from the target to identify its location. This multi-baseline positioning method offers greater flexibility and better performance than single-baseline methods, which have been widely applied in sensor networks, radar, sonar, and MIMO radar. Multi-baseline systems with multiple transmitters can produce excellent positioning accuracy, superior observability, and broad coverage of target range.
In a multi-baseline setup, there may be two types of measurement values between an emission - reception pair. One is the indirect path time measurement value generated by the signal propagation from the transmitter to the receiver through the target reflection, and the other is the direct path time measurement value between the transmitter and receiver via line of sight propagation. Only the indirect measurement value is relevant to the desired target position, defining an ellipse (2D) or surface (3D), with the target located on an ellipse centered at the focus of the transmitter and receiver positions. The ellipses from multiple emission - reception pairs intersect, yielding an estimate of the target position. Multi-baseline positioning methods are sometimes referred to as ellipsoidal positioning.
Elongation positioning has always been an active research field, with numerous results in the literature. Recently, [28] conducted a thorough study on the achievable precision of elongation positioning, and also derived the optimal receiver layout when the number of receivers is even. [29] extended the optimal geometric analysis to more general cases by minimizing the area of the estimated confidence region, which is applicable to both even and odd numbers of receivers. Regarding the estimation of target positions from elongation measurements, [30] studied spherical intersection and spherical interpolation methods, but their positioning performance was only suboptimal. [31] proposed a BLUE (Best Likely Uncertain) estimator by expanding the measurement equation using Taylor series expansion, which requires accurate initial guesses. [32] introduced a two-step estimator that is closed-form when there is only one transmitter. [33] developed a similar closed-form solution for indoor positioning scenarios. [34] and [35] extended the two-step estimator to multiple transmitters, where the former used a multidimensional scaling (MDS) framework, and the latter applied weighted least squares (WLS) optimization as in [14]. [36] derived an algebraic solution for sonar applications where the speed of signal propagation is uncertain. In the literature on elongation positioning, it is generally assumed that the transmitter positions are available, although there may be some errors.
This paper addresses the problem of ellipsoid positioning scenarios where the transmitter's location is completely unavailable. This scenario occurs when the transmitter is located in some special and inaccessible place, as well as in passive coherent positioning systems where the illumination signal comes from some unknown wireless power source. Another situation is that the position of the transmitter changes over time, and its reported / estimated position is unreliable. This is especially true in sonar because the transmitter may drift with the currents, making previous estimates of the transmitter's position irrelevant. In fact, the position of the transmitter can even be deliberately unknown. Under this configuration, the transmitter operates solely as a source of illumination, simplifying its structure significantly and reducing hardware and implementation costs. For example, it allows underwater wireless sensor networks to alleviate the burden of surface buoys in determining and sending their own positions. A straightforward strategy to address this issue of positioning without the transmitter's location is to perform a differential between two indirect measurements, resulting in a measurement equation similar to Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA), which is independent of the transmitter's position since the propagation time between the transmitter and the target is common across all indirect path measurements. The measured values define a hyperboloid rather than an ellipse for positioning.
This paper addresses the problem of target position estimation in a given scenario by employing different methods, specifically joint estimation of target and transmitter positions. Despite the transmitter position being not of interest, we utilize both indirect and direct path measurement values for joint estimation. By leveraging the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) to study the advantages of joint estimation and the improvement in positioning accuracy compared to differential methods, we demonstrate the CRLB performance in a small error region under Gaussian noise. We then develop an algebraic closed-form solution to address the highly nonlinear joint estimation problem and analyze its performance in achieving the CRLB within a Gaussian noise environment. The solution is extended to scenarios where sensor position has random errors and multiple unknown transmitters exist. Finally, we derive the optimal receiver layout for elliptical positioning when the transmitter position is unknown, taking into account the optimization criteria for the estimation confidence region and the mean square error (MSE) of target position estimation, which yield slightly different configurations. The optimal layout allows us to characterize the loss in best possible performance due to the unknown transmitter position.

Conclusion: This paper investigates the problem of target positioning in multi-base station systems where the transmitter position is unavailable. Starting from the basic research through the Cramér-Rao lower bound, we show that there is performance improvement over using only the indirect measurements or introducing a new variable for the transmitter - target distance via the hyperbolic method. An algebraic closed-form solution is proposed to solve the nonlinear joint estimation problem, and first-order analysis confirms the achievement of the CRLB performance in a small error region under Gaussian noise. The algorithm is extended to consider receiver position errors and situations with multiple unknown transmitters. We also derive the optimal receiver layout for this positioning system in a two-dimensional scenario when the number of receivers is even. When the optimal receiver layout standard is minimizing the estimation confidence region, the best achievable performance loss is 3dB; when it is minimizing the estimation MSE, the loss is 1.16dB.
The proposed positioning method assumes that the transmitter is cooperative, thus providing a timestamp in the transmitted signal to allow the receiver to obtain both indirect and direct path distance measurements. In cases where the transmitter is not intentional, such as in passive coherent systems, it is generally unknown when the signal is transmitted. If the transmitted signal has a well-defined pattern, such as certain standard synchronization or pilot sequences, it is still possible to estimate indirect and direct path distances, but an unknown constant offset will be added. The extension of this situation is our future research direction. The method proposed is not applicable to scenarios where the transmitter is non-cooperative, i.e., when there is no timestamp for the transmitted signal and no known pattern.
